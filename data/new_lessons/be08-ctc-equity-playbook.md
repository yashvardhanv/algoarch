# Lesson 08 — Offer Structure and Equity Playbook

> **Priority:** ★★★★☆. Reading an offer by its structure protects you from comparing two headline numbers that mean different things.
>
> **Study time:** 60 minutes. **Do this after:** Lesson 05 (the recruiter screen and negotiation structure) and Lesson 07 (leveling). The level you are offered shapes everything else in the package, so settle it first.
>
> **Level:** extended.

An offer arrives as one headline number and a page of terms. The headline adds things that are
guaranteed to things that may never pay, cash you can spend next month to shares you may not be able
to sell for years. Two offers with the same headline can be very different jobs to hold. This lesson
teaches you to take an offer apart, compare two of them line by line, and negotiate on structure
rather than on a single figure.

> **What this lesson will not do.** It states no salary, equity or tax figures and no "typical"
> ranges. Pay, vesting and tax practices differ by company, by level, by country and by year, and
> they change. Every example uses symbols (A, B, G shares, a price P) or plain index numbers that
> are illustrative and are not money. **Nothing here is tax or legal advice.** For any number you
> act on, use your own offer letter, the company's plan documents, and a qualified tax adviser in
> your country.

---

## 1. Mental Model

> **Every line of an offer has three properties: how certain it is, when it reaches you, and
> whether you can actually spend it. The headline number ignores all three. Your job is to put
> them back.**

Read each component through those three lenses:

| Lens | The question | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Certainty** | Is it guaranteed, or does it depend on ratings, company results or a share price? | A target is a plan, not a promise |
| **Timing** | When does it pay: monthly, yearly, once, or on a vesting schedule? | Money that arrives in year four is not money in year one, and you may leave before it arrives |
| **Liquidity** | Can you turn it into cash you control, and what does it cost you to do so? | Shares in a company with no market for them are worth what you can eventually sell them for, not what a spreadsheet says |

Everything else in this lesson follows from applying these three lenses honestly, to your own
numbers.

---

## 2. Recognise the Component, Ask the Right Question

When you see a line in the offer, reach for the matching question.

| If the offer says... | It usually means... | Ask... |
|---|---|---|
| **Base salary** | Fixed pay on a schedule | Is it the basis for bonus targets and future raises? When is the next review? |
| **Target bonus** | A variable amount tied to your rating and often to company results | How is it decided, and how has the actual payout compared with target recently? Is year one prorated? |
| **Sign-on / joining bonus** | A one-time cash payment | Is there a clawback if I leave within some period? Is it paid in one go or in parts? |
| **RSUs** (restricted stock units) | A promise of shares that you receive as they vest | What is the vesting schedule, how is the number of units set, and when can I sell? |
| **Stock options** | The right to buy shares at a fixed strike price, once vested, until they expire | What is the strike price, the latest valuation, the expiry, and the exercise window after leaving? |
| **Refreshers** | Later equity grants to existing employees | Are they standard or discretionary? How are they decided? |
| **Retirement contributions, insurance, other benefits** | Value you may not be able to spend now | Which parts are counted inside the headline? Which need a minimum service period? |
| **"Total compensation" or "CTC" headline** | A sum of all of the above, valued somehow | Which components, valued at what price, over which year? |

The last row is the one to start with. Ask how the headline was built before you react to it.

---

## 3. Guaranteed, Variable and Illiquid

Sort every component into one of three columns. Most mistakes in offer comparison come from adding
numbers across columns as if they were the same thing.

| Column | What belongs in it | How to treat it |
|---|---|---|
| **Guaranteed cash** | Base salary; sign-on bonus (minus any clawback risk) | Count at face value, after tax, in the year it is paid |
| **Variable cash** | Target bonus; any performance-linked cash | Count at a discount you choose and can defend: what has it actually paid, and how confident are you in your own rating? |
| **Equity** | RSUs, options, refreshers | Count only what vests in the year in question, valued at a price you state, and mark whether you can sell it |

A component that counts toward the headline but that you cannot spend until you leave or retire
(some retirement contributions work this way) sits outside all three columns for the purpose of
"what can I live on". It is still real value; just keep it on its own line.

## 4. Equity Mechanics

### 4.1 Grant, vesting and cliff

A **grant** is the company's promise of equity, written in an agreement that names the number of
units or options, the **vesting schedule**, and, for options, the strike price. Until a unit vests it
is not yours: if you leave before it vests, you forfeit it.

A **vesting schedule** says when parts of the grant become yours. A **cliff** is a period at the
start during which nothing vests; when it ends, the portion for that whole period vests at once and
the rest continues on the schedule.

**Worked example (illustrative schedule, not a norm).** You are granted G units. The schedule vests
a quarter of the grant at the end of each year for four years, with a one-year cliff.

| You leave after | Vested units | What you keep |
|---|---|---|
| 11 months | 0 | Nothing; the cliff has not passed |
| 13 months | G / 4 | The first tranche |
| 3 years | 3G / 4 | Three tranches; the last quarter is forfeited |
| 4 years | G | The whole grant |

Now change one thing: the same G units, but back-loaded so that less vests in the first years and
more in the later ones. The four-year total is identical; what you keep if you leave after two years
is smaller. **Always compare offers on the schedule, not only on the total.**

Schedules, cliffs and the frequency of vesting after the cliff (yearly, quarterly, monthly) vary by
company and sometimes by grant. Read yours.

### 4.2 RSUs

An RSU is a promise to deliver a share (or its value) when it vests. It has no value to you before
it vests, carries no voting rights and usually no dividends until then, and as long as the share
has any value, a vested RSU is worth something. In the United States the value at vesting is
generally taxed as ordinary income in the year it vests, and companies commonly withhold part of the
shares to cover that tax, so you receive fewer shares than vested.

**Worked example.** V units vest when the share price is P (illustrative). Their value at vesting is
V × P. If some of those shares are withheld for tax, you receive the rest. If you then hold and the
price moves to P′, your gain or loss from that point is measured from the value at vesting, not from
zero.

### 4.3 Stock options

An option is the right, not the obligation, to buy a share at a fixed **strike price** (also called
the exercise price) for a limited period. You must **exercise** (pay the strike price) to own the
share. The strike price is often, but not always, set near the share's value on the grant date.
Employee options usually cannot be sold, and unvested options are cancelled if you
leave.

The value that matters is the **spread**: the share's current value minus the strike price.

**Worked example (illustrative).** You hold X vested options with strike K. The latest valuation of a
share is P.

- If P is above K, the spread per share is P − K and the total spread is X × (P − K). To capture it
  you must pay X × K to exercise.
- If P is at or below K, the options have no exercise value today. Exercising would mean paying more
  than the share is worth.
- If the company is private and there is no market for its shares, you may have to pay X × K, and in
  some tax systems owe tax on the spread, while holding shares you cannot sell. That combination is
  the trap.

In the United States, for example, the IRS distinguishes **statutory** options (incentive stock
options and employee stock purchase plans) from **non-statutory** ones. For most non-statutory
options, nothing is taxed at grant, and the spread is counted as income when you exercise. For
incentive stock options, there is generally no regular income at grant or exercise, but alternative
minimum tax can apply in the year of exercise, and how the sale is taxed depends on holding periods.
Other countries have their own rules; the United Kingdom, for instance, has several tax-advantaged
share schemes and treats shares outside them differently.

### 4.4 Refreshers

A refresher is a new grant to someone already employed. It usually vests on its own schedule and
stacks on top of what is still vesting. Whether you get refreshers, how often, and how large they
are depends on the company and on your performance; ask, and do not assume.

**Worked example: the year-five drop.** Your initial grant vests over four years. In year five it
has fully vested. Your equity income in year five is whatever your refreshers deliver that year, and
nothing from the initial grant. If refreshers are small or discretionary, your pay can fall in year
five even with no change in your job. Model it before you sign, not when it happens.

### 4.5 Paper value and liquidity

**Paper value** is what the equity would be worth if you could sell it today at the price someone
quoted. It becomes real only when two things are true: the shares are vested (and, for options,
exercised), and there is a way to sell them.

| Situation | How to treat its value |
|---|---|
| Listed company, vested shares, no trading restriction for you | Close to cash, at the market price, but it moves |
| Listed company, but you face trading windows or a lock-up | Real, but you choose when you can sell less freely |
| Private company, vested shares or options | Paper value. Ask how and when employees have been able to sell, if ever |
| Unvested anything | Not yours yet. It depends on you staying and the price holding |

The price used in an offer is a choice the company made. Ask which price (today's market price, an
average, the latest private valuation) and when it was set.

### 4.6 Taxes: timing matters, rules vary

You do not need to be a tax expert to compare offers, but you do need to know that **the moment a
tax is due differs by instrument and by country**: at grant, at vesting, at exercise, or at sale.
Two consequences follow:

1. You can owe tax on equity before you can sell it (options exercised in a private company are the
   usual case).
2. The same grant can leave you with different after-tax amounts in different countries.

Some systems also offer elections or deferrals for particular cases (the US, for example, has a
deferral election for qualifying employees of some private companies). Whether any of this applies
to you is a question for a tax adviser in your country, with your plan documents in hand. **This is
not tax advice.**

## 5. Comparing Two Offers by Structure

Do not compare headlines. Build a table per year for each offer, in your own numbers, with the three
columns from §3.

**Worked example in index numbers (not money).** Set offer A's base to 100 as an index. Everything
else is stated relative to it, and the ratios between lines are invented to make the arithmetic
clear. They are not typical of any company or market.

| Year 1 | Offer A (listed company) | Offer B (private company) |
|---|---|---|
| Base (guaranteed) | 100 | 90 |
| Sign-on (guaranteed, clawback if you leave early) | 10 | 0 |
| Target bonus (variable) | 15 | 0 |
| Equity vesting this year, at the quoted price | 25 (RSUs, sellable) | 60 (options, private, spread at latest valuation) |
| **Headline** | **150** | **150** |

The headlines are equal. Now apply the lenses:

- **Guaranteed cash:** A gives 110, B gives 90. If your fixed costs need more than 90, B is a
  problem whatever else it offers.
- **Variable cash:** A's 15 is a target. Discount it by your honest estimate of the payout; you
  choose the discount and write down why.
- **Equity:** A's 25 can be sold as it vests (subject to trading rules), and it moves with the market.
  B's 60 is a spread on paper: you would pay the strike to exercise, may owe tax on exercise
  depending on where you live, and cannot sell until there is a market. Ask yourself what B's 60 is
  worth to you if it takes years to sell, or never sells.
- **Later years:** if A's initial grant vests over four years with refreshers you cannot count on,
  and B's options are all granted up front, both offers can look very different in year five.

The decision is not "which number is bigger"; it is **which risk you are being paid to take, and
whether you can afford it**. A candidate with savings and a belief in company B may rationally
choose B. A candidate who needs predictable cash should value B's equity close to zero until it is
sellable.

with equity shaded by liquidity, showing the year-five drop when only the initial grant is counted.

## 6. Negotiation Structure and Trade-offs

### 6.1 What can move

Treat everything as a question, not an assumption. The components worth asking about are the
level, base salary, sign-on bonus, the size of the equity grant, the start date, and, less often,
the vesting schedule and bonus target. Which of these a given company will move is its policy, and
you find out only by asking; asking costs you little. When one component is fixed, ask whether a
different one can move instead: if base cannot change, perhaps a sign-on or the grant can.

### 6.2 Sequencing

1. **Level first.** It sets the frame the package is built in. Arguing over pay inside the wrong
   level is arguing over the wrong frame.
2. **Understand before you counter.** Get the full structure in writing: every component, the
   vesting schedule, the price used for the equity, bonus history, clawback terms, the deadline.
3. **Counter once, on the whole package.** Say what you would need across components to accept.
   Negotiating one line at a time invites a trade-off on the next line.
4. **Get the final terms in writing before you accept.**

### 6.3 Leverage, described honestly

Your leverage is your real alternatives and the company's wish to close: another written offer,
staying in a job you are happy in, or a clear reason the role is worth more (the level you were
assessed at, scope that grew during the process). Never invent an offer, a number or a deadline.
A competing offer can be checked (the simplest check is "can you share it in writing?"), and a bluff
that is called can cost you the offer you had. If
your only alternative is no alternative, negotiate on information and structure (questions,
timing, which component moves) rather than on threats.

### 6.4 Language that works

Fill the brackets from your own offer and your own research; never quote a number you cannot support.

> "Thank you, I'm excited about the team. Before I respond, could you send the full breakdown in
> writing: each component, the vesting schedule, the share price used for the equity and when it was
> set, and any clawback terms?"

> "I'm comparing this with another offer whose structure is different. To accept, I would need
> [the change, across components]. If that's possible, I'm ready to sign."

> "I want to give this the consideration it deserves. Can the deadline move to [date]?"

### 6.5 What to avoid

- Accepting verbally and then reopening the terms.
- Comparing your gross headline against another offer's after-tax figure, or the reverse.
- Counting unvested or private-company equity as if it were cash.
- Negotiating by email chains that change one component at a time.

## 7. Questions to Ask the Recruiter

Ask them in writing if you can, and keep the answers.

1. Which components make up the headline number, and at what share price is the equity valued?
2. What is the vesting schedule, including any cliff and the frequency after it?
3. Are refreshers standard, and how are they decided?
4. How is the bonus decided, and how has actual payout compared with target recently? Is year one
   prorated?
5. Does the sign-on bonus have a clawback, and on what terms?
6. For options: what is the strike price, the latest valuation and its date, the expiry, and how long
   do I have to exercise vested options if I leave?
7. For a private company: have employees been able to sell shares, and how?
8. Which benefits are counted in the headline, and do any need a minimum service period?
9. What is the level, and what does the next level up require?
10. What is the deadline, and can it move?

---

## 8. Pitfalls

1. **Comparing headlines.** Two equal headlines can hide very different amounts of guaranteed cash.
2. **Counting the whole grant as year-one pay.** Only what vests in a year belongs in that year.
3. **Ignoring the cliff.** Leaving a month before it means leaving with no equity.
4. **Treating options like RSUs.** An option has a strike price to pay and can be worth nothing;
   an RSU is worth something while the share is.
5. **Believing the paper price.** A private valuation is not a sale price, and a listed price moves.
6. **Forgetting the year-five drop.** When the initial grant ends, only refreshers remain.
7. **Exercising without a tax plan.** In some systems you owe tax on the spread at exercise, before
   you can sell.
8. **Missing the clawback.** A sign-on you must repay if you leave early is a loan with conditions.
9. **Bluffing.** An invented competing offer that gets checked can cost you the real one.
10. **Using someone else's country's rules.** Tax and vesting conventions differ; check yours.
