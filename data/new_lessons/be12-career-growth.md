# Lesson 12 — Career Growth: Promotion Packets, Sponsors and Visibility

> **Priority:** ★★☆☆☆. Not an interview topic in itself, but it shapes which stories you collect, how you answer "where do you want to be in five years", and whether you reach the level you are interviewing for.
>
> **Study time:** 60 minutes. **Do this after:** Lessons 09 to 11. You need to know what Staff-level work is before you can document it.
>
> **Level:** advanced. Optional for a crash plan.

Past Senior, most companies stop running a promotion pipeline for you. Larson's survey of Staff engineers found about two-thirds were promoted at the company they were already at and the rest changed companies to reach the title. Larson's advice for both paths is the same: past the career level, take more deliberate control of your own progression. This lesson covers the tools the published guides describe for that: a promotion packet you keep as a map, a sponsor who speaks for your work, visibility that does not hog the room, and the honest limits of all three. It is mostly about work you do before and between interviews.

> **What this lesson will not do.** It does not promise a promotion, tell you how any company's process works, or say anything about pay. It states no compensation figures, and the packet template below carries placeholders instead of numbers. The scenarios are **illustrative**. The sources are practitioner essays and one public career framework, not studies, and the lesson says where a claim is one author's survey.

---

## 1. Mental Model

> **A promotion is a team activity. You supply the evidence, a sponsor carries it into rooms you are not in, and the organisation's own definition of "Staff" decides whether the evidence counts.**

| Piece | What it is | Who owns it |
|---|---|---|
| **The packet** | A living record of Staff-level impact, used as a map before it is a formal submission | You |
| **The sponsor** | Someone who speaks for your work where decisions are made, usually your direct manager, plus ideally someone further up | They, activated by you |
| **Visibility** | People who decide know who you are and what you did, without you consuming all the attention | You, within limits |
| **The fit** | Whether your company has a place for the Staff shape you are | The company; you assess it |

Two honest framing points from the sources. First, Larson says opportunity is unevenly distributed in any company and it is much simpler to align with the unspoken currents than to reroute the river; he also says to assess how fixable the unevenness is. Second, the Dropbox framework, a published ladder, states that promotions recognise sustained impact at the next level, that there is no checkbox set that guarantees one, and that they do not promote on hope. A good packet cannot rescue missing impact; it can only stop real impact going unseen.

## 2. Recognise What You Need

| If this is true of you... | Likely missing piece | Start with |
|---|---|---|
| You are doing Staff-shaped work but no one in a decision room knows it | Visibility | §5 |
| You have visible, strong work and keep hearing generic advice ("do bigger projects") | A sponsor, not more work | §4 |
| You are unsure what Staff means at your company | A packet and a conversation with your manager | §3 |
| You do a lot of coordination and enabling work, and it is not counted | Framing the glue work | §6 |
| Your manager just changed | A refreshed packet review with the new manager | §3.2 |
| You have done the above for a long time and nothing moves | A fit question: this company or another | §7 |

## 3. The Promotion Packet

### 3.1 What goes in it

Larson recommends starting a packet long before you expect to be promoted, the way you might keep a brag document, so it becomes the map to your goal rather than a capstone. Your company will have its own format and you will eventually translate; do not rush that. His general template asks seven questions:

| Question | What it forces you to write |
|---|---|
| **Your Staff projects** | What you did, the impact against a well-defined goal, what made it complex; short, with links to the design docs |
| **High-leverage improvements** | How you improved the organisation, beyond a project |
| **Quantifiable impact** | The measurable effect of the projects |
| **Mentoring** | Who, and through what accomplishments |
| **Glue work** | What you do for the organisation and what difference it made |
| **Advocates** | Which teams and leaders know and support your work, what they value, in one sentence with data if possible |
| **Gaps** | Real or perceived skill or behaviour gaps, with how you would address each in a sentence |

### 3.2 How to iterate on it

Visual (planned): a timeline of the packet loop, from first one-hour draft through peer edit, manager edit and periodic review, with a marker where a manager change resets progress unless the packet is reviewed with the new manager.

His sequence, in short: decide why you want this (many people choose not to; you need a reason or you may land in a job you dislike); temper expectations, because promotions at this level are built over quarters and years; bring your manager in and say that Staff is your goal; write a first version in an hour; reread it two days later; share it with trusted peers, preferably already Staff-plus; share it with your manager and ask specifically for gaps; then review it periodically with your manager, especially after a manager change, which otherwise tends to reset progress.

### 3.3 A packet entry, illustrative

*Illustrative scenario: an engineer who led a reliability effort across three teams. The bracketed items are placeholders you fill from your own evidence; never fill them with a guess.*

> **Project.** Cut paging noise for the three teams that share the ingestion pipeline. Goal set with the teams' managers: [target metric, agreed before work began].
> **What I did.** Wrote the diagnosis and the rule for what may page ([link to doc]); ran the migration plan; moved the alerts of two teams myself and coached the third.
> **Result.** [measured change in pages per shift, with the date range]. [One sentence on what is still not fixed.]
> **Why complex.** Three owners with different priorities; the metric needed new instrumentation; the rule changed two teams' on-call habits.
> **Advocates.** [Names of the three on-call leads and what each said, as given to me.]
> **Gap I am working on.** I escalate to my manager late; I now send a weekly note.

Two things this does: it names the complexity (not only the outcome), and it ends with a real gap. A packet with no gaps is not credible to the reader.

> **Interview link.** The packet is also your story bank for Lessons 02 and 07. Each entry is a candidate Staff story: the decision, its loser, what changed afterwards. Keep the entries and the stories in step.

## 4. Sponsors

### 4.1 Sponsor versus mentor

Lara Hogan's essay on sponsorship draws the line this way: a sponsor takes responsibility for someone's advancement and puts their name forward when visible, valuable work is handed out, where a mentor mainly gives advice. (Larson makes the related point that a sponsor in a room is spending social capital and being judged on how you perform there; that cost is his observation, not Hogan's.) She notes that you can be sponsored without knowing it, and argues that people from under-represented groups often need opportunity and visibility more than advice. She also notes that the people whose names we raise tend to be those closest to us, which is a bias to counter deliberately.

Larson's reading for promotion: the person speaking up for your work in forums of influence and when advocating for scarce resources. For a Staff promotion that is almost always your direct manager, who turns your packet into the company format and argues for it in the room where others test your qualifications.

### 4.2 Do you need more than one?

If your manager has never promoted anyone to Staff-plus, they may misstep. Larson advises building a relationship further up the chain, so that your skip-level could recall your impact in a meeting two months from now. A well-known failure he describes: engineers who did the work and have visibility are told to complete a Staff project or create space for others, when what they lacked was someone pushing for recognition of what they had already done.

### 4.3 Activating a sponsor

| Do | Why |
|---|---|
| **State the goal** | "I am looking to be recognised as a Staff engineer" tells them what to look for |
| **Ask how to support their sponsorship** | Sponsors have more standing than bandwidth; align the pieces for them |
| **Ask specific, hard-to-fudge questions** | Generic questions get generic answers like "work on larger projects" |
| **Allow "I don't know"** | Pressing for an answer gets invented advice |
| **Build the relationship over time** | Showing up only before decision week is noticed |

Larson's example of a good specific question: describe one project you thought was Staff-level, what went wrong, and ask how you could have handled it better. It is easier to answer than "what should I do to get promoted?" because it hands the answerer the context.

**If it is not working.** He argues that if your manager and you do not work well together, you are unlikely to be promoted, because the manager so directly shapes your actual and perceived impact. He cautions against switching teams or companies at the first friction (transfers usually need your manager's approval, and you lose the chance to learn to work with people you do not click with) and suggests that after about six months of honest effort it is reasonable to consider moving.

## 5. Getting in the Room, and Visibility

### 5.1 Rooms

Larson's framing: there is no single room; entering rooms is an ongoing challenge. To enter you need to bring something useful that the room does not already have, a sponsor, and the sponsor needs to know you want to be there. Things that lower the cost of including you: staying aligned with your manager, optimising for the group, speaking clearly and concisely, being low friction, coming prepared, and following up. Things that get you removed: misunderstanding the room's purpose, being dogmatic, withholding consent, taking all the oxygen, embarrassing your sponsor, and not showing up. His final point is worth keeping: leave rooms that are not useful, and sponsor someone else into the seat.

### 5.2 Visibility

| Kind | Examples from the guide | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Internal** | Long-lived documents such as design docs and specs; leading architecture reviews and forums; cheering on your team's and peers' work; weekly notes anyone can read; office hours | Pick a mix that suits you and does not overload the audience |
| **Executive** | A relationship with your manager's manager, since people at that layer tend to be in the promotion room and rarely back work they do not know | Visibility comes from the work and the packet, not from pitching |
| **External** | Talks, podcasts, a site or a mailing list | Optional; many Staff engineers have none |

The most important sentence in the guide: the best single way to be visible is to work on what matters to your company and its leaders. His advice on how much: use the packet exercise to find out whether lack of visibility is likely to hold you back, clear that threshold, and not much further, because visibility is a transient currency and learning is a permanent one. The guide also notes that organisations that rely on whoever advertises themselves most tend to disadvantage others, and inclusive ones design ways for the whole pool of potential leaders to be seen.

## 6. Staff Projects, Glue Work and Managing the Narrative

**Staff projects.** Larson's survey finds most engineers did not complete a single "Staff project" to reach the role, though a large minority did, particularly those promoted internally. Some companies require one informally as a "soft gate". His characteristics of such a project: complex and ambiguous, many divided stakeholders, and a named bet where failure is visible. Maintaining your packet early is his advice for uncovering unwritten requirements, rather than discovering them when a "sure thing" promotion fails. His summary: if you are seeking an internal promotion and have not held a Staff or management title before, you will likely need one; in other cases you likely will not. Either way he calls them some of the most challenging work available and irreplaceable for long-term growth, even if avoiding one might be quicker for the title.

**Glue work.** Tanya Reilly's talk describes glue work as the needed, often invisible work that makes a team succeed: noticing who is blocked, reviewing designs for hand-waving, onboarding, improving process. She says it is technical leadership, that people are not always rewarded for it, and that doing it too early or without direction can be career limiting. In her illustrative story (she notes it is a composite) the engineer is told her work was not a technical contribution, and she says the manager shares responsibility because the expectation was never made explicit. Use that in practice by asking your manager, explicitly and early: "Which of the things I do does the promotion process count?"

**A caution about narrative.** Your packet should describe glue work in outcomes (what failed to happen, how long people waited), not in adjectives. This is a framing choice, not an inflation of what you did.

## 7. When the Fit Is the Problem

Larson says to take the idea seriously that the fault may lie with the company, not the packet. Opportunity can concentrate where leadership thinks work is "more complex" (for example, infrastructure over product), reward fixing an outage over preventing one, and favour people who are physically near leadership. He also notes that Staff-plus titles are leadership positions, and it is harder to be seen as a candidate if you do not resemble the existing leadership. He says to take the experiences of people who find this seriously, and that there are many role models who made it anyway.

Practical consequences:

- If after a sustained, honest effort (a packet, a sponsor, visible work on what matters) there is no path, it is a legitimate reason to look elsewhere. Lesson 09's archetypes help you ask which kind of Staff role a company actually has.
- If you do change companies, the packet entries become your interview stories. That is another reason to keep them factual.
- Larson also describes engineering management as a possible detour, not a one-way door; several people he interviewed moved between both. Do it for the work, not only for the title, he says, since managing affects other people.

## 8. Pitfalls

1. **A packet written the week before.** It will read like an archive of what you remember, not a map.
2. **Telling your manager what you think they want to hear.** Larson's interviewee said this was a mistake they made in one-on-ones; be honest about what you want.
3. **A packet with no gaps.** The reader will not believe it.
4. **Treating the sponsor as someone who will do the work for you.** Align the pieces for them.
5. **Asking vague questions.** You will get vague advice.
6. **Visibility for its own sake.** Clear the threshold, then stop.
7. **Showing up only at promotion time.**
8. **Leaving at the first friction** with a manager.
9. **Counting on one big project.** The ladder you have read wants sustained impact.
10. **Unmeasured glue work.** Write it as outcomes.
11. **Inventing a metric for the packet.** A placeholder you fill from evidence is honest; a made-up number is not.

## Drills

1. What is the difference between a mentor and a sponsor? Why does Larson say that for a Staff promotion the sponsor is almost always your direct manager?
2. List the seven questions of the packet template. Which one makes the packet credible to a reader, and why does the lesson say a packet without it is not believed?
3. Why start a packet long before you expect a promotion? Name two benefits from the lesson.
4. Your manager changes. What does the lesson say usually happens to your progress, and what do you do in the first month?
5. Rewrite this question to get a useful answer: "What do I need to do to get promoted?"
6. What does a room need from you before it lets you in, and what gets you removed?
7. How much visibility should you pursue, according to Larson, and why does the lesson treat learning as the permanent currency?
8. When is it reasonable to consider that the fit is the problem rather than your packet? Name two signs from the lesson.

## Homework

**Part A.** Start a packet today using the seven questions. Use placeholders where you do not have evidence, and mark each placeholder with where the evidence would come from. Time-box the first draft to one hour.

**Part B.** Wait two days, reread, edit for clarity and context, and then share it with two peers (at least one already Staff-plus). Record what they added that you had not seen as your strength.

**Part C.** Book a conversation with your manager. Bring the packet, say Staff is a goal, and ask the specific-question version from §4.3 about one of your own projects. Write down the answers and the things they said they did not know.

**Part D.** List the rooms you are in and the rooms you would like to be in. For one of the latter, write what you could bring that it does not have, and who might sponsor you. Separately, list one room you should leave.

**Part E.** Turn two packet entries into two-minute interview stories using the shape from Lesson 07, and check each has a decision, its loser, and a measure.
